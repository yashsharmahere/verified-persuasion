# Run 0 — Intake interview

One sitting, about 30 minutes, before he hears any argument. Derived from
design paper §3 (why beliefs resist facts), §8 Run 0 and §10 (instrument and
demand characteristics).

What this session must produce:

1. Consent, recorded.
2. A baseline 0–100 score on the target statement **and** on the control items.
3. His reasons, **in his own words**, verbatim.

Nothing here argues. If he asks what you think, say you'll tell him after the
project is done.

---

## How to run it

- **Record audio** (ask first). Verbatim quotes are data; memory is not.
- **He writes the scores himself, on paper.** Not said aloud to you. Keep the
  sheet — the day-7 form must use the identical wording.
- **Don't react.** No nodding, no "hmm", no "really?". Neutral "okay, and
  then?" only. Any reaction tells him which answer you want.
- **Don't correct anything he says**, even if it's wrong. Today is listening.
- **Ask, then wait.** Silence after a question gets the second, truer answer.

---

## Part A — Consent (read out, roughly)

> "This is for my course project. It's about how people's views on everyday
> things — food, health, a few other topics — form and change. I'll ask you
> some questions today and record them. Later, you'll talk with a computer
> program, and I'll tell you now: it's a machine, and it will try to change
> your mind about one of these topics. It only says things it can back with a
> named source. There are no right answers. If your view doesn't change at
> all, that's a perfectly good result for the project — I'm measuring honestly,
> not hoping for a number. You can stop any time. Okay?"

Do **not** say which statement is the target, or which direction the program
will argue.

Record: date, consent yes/no, disclosure given yes/no.

---

## Part B — Rate these statements (on paper, before discussion)

> "For each one, write how sure you are that it's true. 0 = definitely false,
> 100 = definitely true, 50 = no idea."

Shuffle the order on the sheet so the food items aren't together.

**Candidate targets** — rate all three now; pick the target afterwards from his
reasons. Whichever you pick already has a baseline.

- T1. A person over 60 can eat the same foods, in the same amounts, as at 30,
  without any extra risk to their health.
- T2. As you get older, you don't need to change what or how much you eat.
- T3. Eating more food is what keeps an older person strong.

T3 is the half CONTEXT.md warns about (protein needs genuinely rise with age).
It's here to see how much of his belief lives there — if T3 is his real belief,
the target needs care, not a direct contest.

**Controls** — unrelated, never argued, same scale:

- C1. Reading in dim light permanently damages your eyesight.
- C2. Cold weather by itself causes colds.
- C3. Most petrol cars will be gone from Indian roads within 15 years.
- C4. Cracking your knuckles causes arthritis.

If the target moves later and the controls don't, that's signal. If everything
drifts, it's him being agreeable.

---

## Part C — Why he believes it (the core)

Ask about whichever of T1–T3 he scored highest. Go in order; follow-ups in
brackets only if the answer is short.

**What exactly he believes**

1. "Tell me in your own words what you think about how people should eat as
   they get older."
2. "When you say *everything* — what does that include? Sweets? Fried food?
   Salt? Big portions?" *(This is where it splits. Write down exactly which.)*
3. "Is there anything an older person *should* eat less of?"

**Where it came from** (§3 reason 1 — never encountered the counter-evidence)

4. "How long have you thought this? Where did it come from?" [Your parents?
   Someone you know? Something you read or saw?]
5. "Has anyone ever told you otherwise? What did they say?" *(If no one
   has — the belief is unexamined, and new facts may be enough.)*

**Who he trusts** (§3 reason 2 — discounted the source)

6. "When it comes to food and health, whose word do you trust?" [A doctor?
   Which one? Government advice? Family? Newspapers? Ayurveda? WhatsApp?]
7. "Whose advice on food do you *not* trust? Why?" *(The system must use
   sources he already accepts. This answer decides retrieval.)*

**What the belief does for him** (§3 reason 3 — the belief is doing work)

8. "What would it mean for you if you had to start eating differently?"
9. "Do you feel your age changes anything about you? Energy, strength?"
   *(Listen for identity: "I've always been strong", "I'm not old". If it's
   there, the system contests the claim, never the value. §3: value-threat is
   where backfire actually happens.)*

**Evidence and stakes**

10. "What do you see in your own life that tells you you're right?"
11. "Is there anything that would make you think differently? What would you
    need to see?" *(The single most useful answer for building the argument.)*
12. "Has a doctor ever said anything to you about your diet?" *(See the
    ethics note below before using the answer.)*

**Close — the baseline open response**

13. "In one or two sentences: why do you hold this view?" — **write it down
    word for word.** At the end and at day 7 you'll ask "What, if anything,
    changed your thinking?" and compare. A number that moves without the
    reasoning moving is compliance (§10).

---

## Ethics note

If he has a diagnosed condition (diabetes, BP, heart, kidney) or a doctor has
given him specific diet instructions, the system must not argue around that
advice. Its job is narrower: what the sources say about the general claim.
Anything about his own condition is "ask your doctor." Note this in the case
study.

---

## Afterwards — send these to build on

- Scores for T1–T3 and C1–C4.
- Which target you choose, and its exact wording.
- Verbatim answers to Q2, Q6, Q7, Q9, Q11 and Q13.
- Anything from Q12 that limits what the system may say.

Then retrieval, the conversation loop and the brochure get built around his
actual reasons and his trusted sources.
